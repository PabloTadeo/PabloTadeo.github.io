About

I build the tools I needed and could not find.

I am from Xalapa, Veracruz, and I came into sport science from the field rather than from a laboratory. As a physical education graduate there I kept running into the same wall: the numbers we used to judge athletes were rarely as solid as the decisions we made with them. That question has organized everything since.

Today I am a professor at the Faculty of Sport Organization, Universidad Autonoma de Nuevo Leon, and the lead data analyst of the TigreFit health program. My research sits where artificial intelligence, data science and applied statistics meet exercise science: validating the AI and computer vision tools the field has adopted faster than it has tested them, modeling how young athletes actually develop across thousands of repeated measures, and extending the same analytical rigor to populations that health research usually leaves out of its samples.

The part I care about most is access. Statistical software and scientific writing arrive to most students as walls, and to most coaches as a black box. So I build things that lower those walls: reproducible pipelines, teaching applications, AI agents that take the mechanical work off the table so the thinking gets the time. Complex methods are worth little if the people who need them cannot use them.

I work across four countries and three languages, and I am at my best in the room where a biomechanist, a statistician and a coach have to agree on what the data actually says.

Beyond the lab

I spent years marching with a drum on my chest.

Before I ever ran a statistical model I played percussion in the Delfines Marching Band, and in 2015 we marched the Rose Parade in Pasadena, California. Two hundred people, five miles of street, and a piece of music that only works if every one of them holds the same tempo.

I still think it was the best training I have had. A marching band is a system where individual excellence counts for nothing unless it synchronizes with everyone else, where you repeat something a thousand times so it survives the one time that matters, and where the audience only ever sees the result. Research works the same way, and so does a laboratory full of students.

It is also the first place where Mexico and the United States stopped being two separate contexts for me and became one stage.

Research program

The method comes before the finding.

Human movement generates more data than the field knows how to model. My research builds the methods that close that gap: computer vision and machine learning to capture movement, multilevel and longitudinal statistics to explain it, and reproducible infrastructure so the analysis survives contact with the next dataset.

01

AI and computer vision for movement assessment

Validation of AI-driven smartphone applications, computer vision pipelines, IMU-based accelerometry and optical timing against force platforms. Includes algorithmic fairness: whether automated detection degrades with skin pigmentation under controlled lighting.

Computer vision · Signal processing · Measurement validity
02

Statistical modeling of youth development

Mixed-longitudinal and multilevel models of stretch-shortening cycle function across ages 5 to 20, separating maturation from chronological age in braking mechanics and force production, on repeated-measures data at population scale.

Linear mixed models · GAMM · Developmental trajectories
03

Data science for population health

Cardiometabolic risk profiling and diagnostic reclassification in underserved populations, including indigenous older adults in Mexico and Colombia, where the analytical question is how classification thresholds themselves reshape who counts as at risk.

Epidemiological modeling · Diagnostic reclassification · Health equity
04

Reproducible research infrastructure

Automated analysis pipelines in R and Quarto, version-controlled and archived, plus a set of AI agents that handle the mechanical layer of the research workflow so statistical judgment gets the time it needs.

R · Quarto · Git · LLM agent orchestration
Selected work

Three papers that define the line.

One key figure from each. Together they cover what the research program does: test whether AI measures what it claims, benchmark it against laboratory reference standards, and model what happens when the same instrumentation is deployed at population scale.

01 · Algorithmic fairness
Validity and reliability of an AI-based jump height app vs. infrared contact mat
Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research · Q1
Excellent consistency against the infrared mat (ICC 0.93 SJ, 0.92 CMJ) alongside a systematic overestimation of roughly 4.2 cm, and no meaningful degradation across skin pigmentation under standardized lighting.
Read the paper
02 · Measurement validity
Reliability and validity of an AI-driven smartphone application for countermovement jump height
Measurement in Physical Education and Exercise Science · Q1
Across five simultaneous instruments, within-session reliability was near-perfect for the force platform, slow-motion video and infrared mat (ICC ≈ 0.99) and lower for the AI application (ICC ≈ 0.96). Between sessions the gap widened: the AI app fell to ICC ≈ 0.90 with a wide confidence interval, while every reference method stayed above 0.96.
Read the paper
03 · Population-scale modeling
Normative performance values and sex-based developmental trajectories in Mexican school sport programs
Frontiers in Sports and Active Living · Q1
Builds the first longitudinal normative reference for Mexican school sport using force platform instrumentation, turning routine testing into a developmental model.

AI tools, agents and automation

Twenty-five agents, one system.

Every stage of the research pipeline runs through a network of specialized AI agents I built and maintain. They are not isolated tools: the output of one is the input of the next, from raw literature and data through statistics, figures, drafting and pre-submission audit, version-controlled and reproducible from source.

Agents are grouped by function, but connected end to end. Nothing in the pipeline is a dead end.
The VALD integration at UANL: I sit between the instrumentation and the research team, turning raw device output into modeled, shareable evidence.

I lead an exploratory research program on quality of life and physical activity among indigenous older adults, spanning northeastern, central and southern Mexico, and extending to Pueblos Originarios in Colombia in partnership with Uniautonoma del Cauca in the Popayan region. The purpose is straightforward: generate peer-reviewed evidence about a population that health research has largely excluded from its samples.

The program has already produced a published PRISMA 2020 systematic review on physical activity and modifiable risk factors in Mexican indigenous peoples, and a multicentric observational protocol on functional health.
